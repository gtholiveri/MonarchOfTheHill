\section{Drivetrain}
\label{sec:drivetrain}

The drivetrain has to carry a \qty{0.97}{kg} robot up the \ang{33.4} slope as fast as possible. The kit motors were too slow for that, so we bought inexpensive gearmotors. We chose them by first bounding the options with three physical limits: the traction the tracks can transmit (Section~\ref{sec:traction-bound}), the force needed to climb (Section~\ref{sec:holding-bound}) and the current the electronics can supply (Section~\ref{sec:current-bound}). We then picked the gear ratio with the shortest predicted climb among the motors left (Section~\ref{sec:gear-ratio}).

\subsection{Upper bound on torque: traction}
\label{sec:traction-bound}

The tracks can push along the ramp only until they slip, at the static friction force $F_f$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:fbd}). The total drive force $F$ is therefore capped by the maximum available traction:
\begin{equation}
  F \le \mu_s N
  \label{eq:traction}
\end{equation}
where the normal force is $N = m g \cos\ang{33.4}$ on the ramp and $N = m g$ on the mesa. Each motor drives one track through a sprocket of \qty{50}{mm} diameter ($r = \qty{25}{mm}$) and supplies half of $F$, so the torque per motor at which the tracks start to slip is $\mu_s N r / 2$ at the sprocket. We estimated $\mu_s = 1.2$ to be the most any tread material on the ramp could reasonably give, since that is about the highest static friction coefficient that surfaces engineered for grip, such as tires, reach. On the ramp the maximum available traction is then \qty{9.5}{N}, and the maximum usable torque per motor is \qty{0.12}{N.m} at the sprocket (Table~\ref{tab:traction}).

  \label{fig:fbd}
\end{figure}

A real drivetrain also loses torque to resistance in the tracks, axles and couplers (Section~\ref{sec:holding-bound}), and we could not calculate how much before the drivetrain was built. The motors must supply that loss on top of the force the tracks transmit, so we allowed stall force up to three times the maximum available traction. The factor of 3 is a judgment call, generous on purpose because the losses were unknown. Physically, it means a motor at this bound could lose two thirds of its stall force inside the drivetrain and still deliver the full maximum available traction at stall. Excess stall force is not wasted outright: a motor's force falls as it speeds up (Eq.~\eqref{eq:motor}), so it only makes the tracks slip for the first part of the climb.

\subsection{Lower bound on torque: holding on the slope}
\label{sec:holding-bound}

Gravity pulls the robot down the ramp with $m g \sin\ang{33.4} = \qty{5.24}{N}$. To start from rest and climb, the motors' stall force must exceed that pull plus every other resistance:
\begin{equation}
  F_\mathrm{stall} > k_r\, m g \sin\ang{33.4}
  \label{eq:holding}
\end{equation}
Here $k_r$ is the total resistance to climbing as a multiple of gravity's pull, so $k_r - 1$, which we call the extra resistance, counts every resistance besides gravity and the gearbox losses, which Section~\ref{sec:gear-ratio} counts separately as the gearbox efficiency $\eta_\mathrm{g}$. A value of $k_r = 1$ would be a robot with no extra resistance; $k_r = 1.25$ means the extra resistance adds a quarter of gravity's pull, \qty{1.31}{N}. We could not calculate $k_r$ from first principles, because it comes from friction and deflection throughout the drivetrain and from rubbing on the side wall (Section~\ref{sec:drivetrain-result}). A motor that only just clears Eq.~\eqref{eq:holding} climbs, but with so little force to spare for acceleration it never reaches its full speed on a \qty{1.524}{m} ramp.

\subsection{Upper bound on motor size: current}
\label{sec:current-bound}

A bigger motor makes more power only by drawing more current. A brushed DC motor with winding resistance $R_\mathrm{w}$ stalls at $I_\mathrm{stall} = V / R_\mathrm{w}$ and delivers its peak mechanical power at half its stall torque and half its no-load speed:
\begin{equation}
  P_\mathrm{max} = \frac{V I_\mathrm{stall}}{4}
  \label{eq:pmax}
\end{equation}
The voltage is fixed by the kit's 2S pack, so stall current is a proxy for motor size: the largest viable motors are those whose current the electronics can supply. The motors will stall briefly at the start of every climb and could stall for a long time in a pushing match. Two components limit $I_\mathrm{stall}$ (Table~\ref{tab:current}).

\paragraph{Driver}
The TB6612FNG driver we had on hand is rated for \qty{1.2}{A} continuous and \qty{3.2}{A} peak per channel~\cite{tb6612}. A continuous stall, though unlikely, could occur in a pushing match, which caps $I_\mathrm{stall}$ at \qty{1.2}{A} per motor. Because most stalls are brief, up to \qty{3.2}{A} is acceptable.

\paragraph{Pack}
The 7805 linear regulator steps an input between 7 and \qty{25}{V} down to the \qty{5}{V} the Photon runs on~\cite{reg7805}. Below \qty{7}{V} input, the \qty{5}{V} supply cannot be held and the Photon behaves erratically or cuts out, which loses the match. The voltage sag from the motors in a likely worst case therefore must not pull the pack below \qty{7}{V}. The terminal voltage of a battery with open-circuit voltage $\mathcal{E}$ and internal resistance $R_\mathrm{int}$ is
\begin{equation}
  V_\mathrm{term} = \mathcal{E} - I_\mathrm{pack} R_\mathrm{int}
  \label{eq:vterm}
\end{equation}
so with both motors at stall the pack sags by $\Delta V_\mathrm{sag} = 2 I_\mathrm{stall} R_\mathrm{int}$, and the largest stall current per motor that keeps the pack above \qty{7}{V} is
\begin{equation}
  I_\mathrm{stall,max} = \frac{\mathcal{E} - \qty{7}{V}}{2 R_\mathrm{int}}
  \label{eq:istall-max}
\end{equation}
Taking $R_\mathrm{int} \approx \qty{0.15}{\ohm}$ \tbd{replace with our measured value from the voltage-drop test} and $\mathcal{E} = \qty{7.4}{V}$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:istall-max} gives $I_\mathrm{stall,max} = \qty{1.3}{A}$. The value \qty{7.4}{V} is the nominal 2S voltage; a 2S pack runs from \qty{8.4}{V} full to a usual cutoff of \qty{6.0}{V}~\cite{cell}. But even with both motors stalled throughout, six \qty{30}{s} runs draw only about \qty{0.14}{A.h}, a few percent of the pack's capacity, so the pack stays near nominal. The best case, a full pack at $\mathcal{E} = \qty{8.4}{V}$, allows \qty{4.7}{A} per motor.

We wanted a motor wound for close to our pack voltage. The match does not have to be exact: above its rated voltage a motor's speed and stall current rise in proportion, so a \qty{6}{V} motor on our \qty{7.4}{V} pack runs about \qty{23}{\percent} faster and draws about \qty{23}{\percent} more current, which Table~\ref{tab:current} must allow. A \qty{37}{mm} gearmotor stalls at \qty{5.5}{A} even at its \qty{12}{V} rating~\cite{pololu37d}, several times our worst-case limit. Pololu's \qty{25}{mm}, \qty{6}{V} gearmotors stall at about \qty{2.0}{A}~\cite{pololu25d}. The cheaper \qty{25}{mm} motors we found on Amazon are rated at \qty{2.7}{W}~\cite{meccanixity}; taking that as the peak power on our pack, Eq.~\eqref{eq:pmax} gives $I_\mathrm{stall} = 4 P_\mathrm{max} / V = \qty{1.46}{A}$, about \qty{1.5}{A}. That is just above the worst-case limit and well inside the best case, so of the sizes we compared, a \qty{25}{mm} gearmotor wound for \qty{6}{V} fits the kit pack best.

\subsection{Choosing the gear ratio inside the bounds}
\label{sec:gear-ratio}

We found affordable \qty{25}{mm} \qty{6}{V} gearmotors on Amazon with ratios from 1:4.4 to 1:500~\cite{meccanixity}. Every version shares the same base motor, so all of them meet Section~\ref{sec:current-bound}. The torque bounds of Sections~\ref{sec:traction-bound} and~\ref{sec:holding-bound} leave three candidates: the 170, 280 and \qty{620}{\rpm} versions (Fig.~\ref{fig:gear}a).

Varying gear ratio on the same base motor only changes how power is allocated between speed and torque, but it is strictly zero-sum with respect to total power. Therefore, choosing a gear ratio is an important tradeoff, with an optimum somewhere in the middle (Table~\ref{tab:tradeoff}).

To find where that optimum lies among the candidates, we wrote the estimated climb time as a function of gear ratio, $t_\mathrm{climb}(G)$, and evaluated it for every version on sale.

\paragraph{Motor model}
We derive the drive force from the motor's two governing equations and the drivetrain geometry, write Newton's second law along the ramp, and solve it for the climb time.

A brushed DC motor makes torque in proportion to its current, and the pack voltage divides between the winding resistance and the back-EMF the spinning motor generates. In SI units the torque constant and the back-EMF constant are the same number, $k_\mathrm{t}$:
\begin{align}
  \tau_\mathrm{m} &= k_\mathrm{t} I_\mathrm{m} \label{eq:torque} \\
  V &= I_\mathrm{m} R_\mathrm{w} + k_\mathrm{t}\, \omega_\mathrm{m} \label{eq:kvl}
\end{align}
Solving Eq.~\eqref{eq:kvl} for $I_\mathrm{m}$ and substituting it into Eq.~\eqref{eq:torque} gives torque in terms of speed:
\begin{equation}
  \tau_\mathrm{m} = \frac{k_\mathrm{t} V}{R_\mathrm{w}} - \frac{k_\mathrm{t}^2}{R_\mathrm{w}}\, \omega_\mathrm{m}
  \label{eq:tau-omega}
\end{equation}
Torque decreases linearly with speed, from its stall value at zero speed to zero at the no-load speed.

Both constants come from those two limits. At no load the current is nearly zero, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:kvl} reduces to $V = k_\mathrm{t}\omega_\mathrm{m}$. At stall the speed is zero, so it reduces to $V = I_\mathrm{m} R_\mathrm{w}$. We had to choose before we had a motor to test, so we took the no-load speed from the listing, about \qty{6000}{\rpm} (\qty{625}{rad/s}) at \qty{6}{V}, and used our estimated stall current of \qty{1.5}{A} on the \qty{7.4}{V} pack (Section~\ref{sec:current-bound}):
\begin{align}
  k_\mathrm{t} &= \frac{\qty{6}{V}}{\qty{625}{rad/s}} = \qty{0.0096}{N.m/A} \label{eq:k} \\
  R_\mathrm{w} &= \frac{\qty{7.4}{V}}{\qty{1.5}{A}} = \qty{4.9}{\ohm} \label{eq:R}
\end{align}

The gearbox divides speed by $G$ and multiplies torque by $G$, losing a fraction of the torque to the gearbox efficiency $\eta_\mathrm{g}$. The sprocket of radius $r$ turns shaft speed into track speed and shaft torque into force:
\begin{align}
  \omega_\mathrm{m} &= G\, \omega_\mathrm{s} \label{eq:gear-speed} \\
  \tau_\mathrm{s} &= \eta_\mathrm{g} G\, \tau_\mathrm{m} \label{eq:gear-torque} \\
  v &= r\, \omega_\mathrm{s} \label{eq:sprocket-speed} \\
  F &= \frac{2\, \tau_\mathrm{s}}{r} \label{eq:sprocket-force}
\end{align}
where the 2 counts both motors. Combining Eqs.~\eqref{eq:gear-speed}--\eqref{eq:sprocket-force}, motor speed and drive force in terms of the robot's speed and the motor torque are
\begin{align}
  \omega_\mathrm{m} &= \frac{G v}{r} \label{eq:omega-v} \\
  F &= \frac{2 \eta_\mathrm{g} G}{r}\, \tau_\mathrm{m} \label{eq:F-tau}
\end{align}
Substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:tau-omega} into Eq.~\eqref{eq:F-tau}, then Eq.~\eqref{eq:omega-v} for $\omega_\mathrm{m}$, gives the drive force as a function of speed:
\begin{equation}
  F(v) = \frac{2 \eta_\mathrm{g} k_\mathrm{t} G V}{R_\mathrm{w} r} - \frac{2 \eta_\mathrm{g} k_\mathrm{t}^2 G^2}{R_\mathrm{w} r^2}\, v
  \label{eq:F-v-expanded}
\end{equation}
The first term is the force at $v = 0$, the stall force. Setting $F = 0$ gives the robot's no-load speed:
\begin{align}
  F_\mathrm{stall} &= \frac{2 \eta_\mathrm{g} k_\mathrm{t} G V}{R_\mathrm{w} r} \label{eq:fstall} \\
  v_0 &= \frac{V r}{k_\mathrm{t} G} \label{eq:v0}
\end{align}
so Eq.~\eqref{eq:F-v-expanded} can be written
\begin{equation}
  F(v) = F_\mathrm{stall}\left(1 - \frac{v}{v_0}\right)
  \label{eq:motor}
\end{equation}
Raising $G$ raises $F_\mathrm{stall}$ and lowers $v_0$ in the same proportion. This is the tradeoff in Table~\ref{tab:tradeoff}.

\paragraph{Equation of motion}
Two kinds of resistance oppose the drive force. Internal losses eat motor torque before it reaches the ramp: friction within the treads, the side guards rubbing on the treads, and the energy spent flexing the couplers as they turn. External resistance acts on the robot directly, as gravity does: friction and small collisions against the side wall. We lump both into $k_r$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:holding}), so the total resistance is
\begin{equation}
  F_\mathrm{r} = k_r\, m g \sin\ang{33.4}
  \label{eq:W}
\end{equation}
The drive force also cannot exceed the maximum available traction, $\mu_s m g \cos\ang{33.4}$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:traction}). For $t_\mathrm{climb}(G)$ we took $\mu_s = 1.0$, the middle row of Table~\ref{tab:traction}. Newton's second law along the ramp is then
\begin{equation}
  m \frac{dv}{dt} = \min\!\left[F(v),\ \mu_s m g \cos\ang{33.4}\right] - F_\mathrm{r}
  \label{eq:climb}
\end{equation}
The model treats the two losses differently. Gear losses grow with the load on the gears, so $\eta_\mathrm{g}$ scales the motor force. Every other resistance is a constant drag, set by $k_r$.

While the tracks slip, the motors make more force than the tracks can use, and in the real robot that surplus covers the internal losses. The model instead subtracts all of $F_\mathrm{r}$ from the maximum available traction, so it predicts a slower start than the real robot's. The model also assumes the motors run at full power from the instant the run starts.

\paragraph{Solving for the climb time}
$t_\mathrm{climb}(G)$ is the time Eq.~\eqref{eq:climb}, started from rest, takes to carry the robot \qty{1.524}{m}. The $\min$ splits the climb into two phases.

In the first phase the tracks slip. At low speed the motors can make more force than the maximum available traction, so the drive force is held at that constant value and the robot accelerates uniformly:
\begin{equation}
  a_1 = \frac{\mu_s m g \cos\ang{33.4} - F_\mathrm{r}}{m}
  \label{eq:a1}
\end{equation}
This phase ends at the speed $v_1$ where $F(v)$ falls to the maximum available traction. It lasts for a time $t_1$ and covers a distance $x_1$:
\begin{align}
  v_1 &= v_0\left(1 - \frac{\mu_s m g \cos\ang{33.4}}{F_\mathrm{stall}}\right) \label{eq:v1} \\
  t_1 &= \frac{v_1}{a_1} \label{eq:t1} \\
  x_1 &= \frac{v_1^2}{2 a_1} \label{eq:x1}
\end{align}
A version whose $F_\mathrm{stall}$ is below the maximum available traction never slips, and skips this phase ($v_1 = t_1 = x_1 = 0$).

In the second phase the motors limit the force. Substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:motor} into Eq.~\eqref{eq:climb} gives a first-order linear differential equation:
\begin{equation}
  m \frac{dv}{dt} = F_\mathrm{stall}\left(1 - \frac{v}{v_0}\right) - F_\mathrm{r}
  \label{eq:phase2}
\end{equation}
Its solution, with $t$ measured from the start of this phase and $v = v_1$ at $t = 0$, approaches exponentially the speed $v_t$ at which the drive force exactly balances the resistance:
\begin{align}
  v(t) &= v_t - (v_t - v_1)\, e^{-t/t_0} \label{eq:v-t} \\
  v_t &= v_0\left(1 - \frac{F_\mathrm{r}}{F_\mathrm{stall}}\right) \label{eq:vt} \\
  t_0 &= \frac{m v_0}{F_\mathrm{stall}} \label{eq:t0}
\end{align}
As a function test, let $F_\mathrm{stall}$ fall toward $F_\mathrm{r}$, the least force that can move the robot up the ramp at all. Then $v_t$ falls to zero and the climb time grows without bound, which is what should happen to a motor that can barely hold the robot in place.
Integrating Eq.~\eqref{eq:v-t} once more gives the position along the ramp:
\begin{equation}
  x(t) = x_1 + v_t t - t_0 (v_t - v_1)\left(1 - e^{-t/t_0}\right)
  \label{eq:x-t}
\end{equation}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:x-t} is the exact solution of Eq.~\eqref{eq:phase2}, so no differential equation solver is needed. It cannot be rearranged for $t$, so a short Python script finds the $t$ at which $x = \qty{1.524}{m}$ with a numerical root finder. Adding $t_1$ gives $t_\mathrm{climb}(G)$.

\paragraph{Why there is an optimum}
For the \qty{280}{\rpm} version ($\eta_\mathrm{g} = 0.7$, $k_r = 1$), the first phase lasts only \qty{0.17}{s} and covers \qty{4}{cm}. After it, the gap between $v$ and $v_t$ shrinks with a time constant $t_0$ of only \qty{0.05}{s}. The robot therefore climbs at close to $v_t$ (\qty{0.63}{m/s}) for nearly the whole ramp, and the gear ratio with the largest $v_t$ gives the fastest climb.

$v_t$ depends on $G$ through $F_\mathrm{stall}$, which is proportional to $G$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:fstall}), and $v_0$, which is proportional to $1/G$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:v0}). Substituting both into Eq.~\eqref{eq:vt} and setting its derivative with respect to $G$ to zero, $v_t$ is largest when
\begin{equation}
  F_\mathrm{stall} = 2 F_\mathrm{r}
  \label{eq:optimum}
\end{equation}
Then $v_t = v_0/2$: the motors climb at half their no-load speed, which is where a DC motor delivers its peak power. For all three combinations of $\eta_\mathrm{g}$ and $k_r$ in Fig.~\ref{fig:gear}b, Eq.~\eqref{eq:optimum} puts the optimum between the \qty{620}{\rpm} (1:9.6) and \qty{280}{\rpm} (1:21.3) versions.

\paragraph{Best version}
The \qty{280}{\rpm} version (1:21.3) gives the shortest estimated climb (Fig.~\ref{fig:gear}b). It stays fastest for every combination of $\eta_\mathrm{g}$ from 0.5 to 0.8 and $k_r$ from 1.0 to 1.25 except the most optimistic corner, $\eta_\mathrm{g}$ of 0.75 or more with $k_r$ of about 1.1 or less, where the \qty{620}{\rpm} version is slightly faster. Nothing is sold between 280 and \qty{620}{\rpm}.

\subsection{Result}
\label{sec:drivetrain-result}

We bought and ran the \qty{280}{\rpm} version. We measured its stall current on a charged pack at \qty{1.4}{A}, close to the \qty{1.5}{A} we had estimated. In three stopwatch trials the robot climbed the ramp in about \qty{3.3}{s}. For the \qty{280}{\rpm} version, $t_\mathrm{climb}(G)$ gives 2.4 to \qty{3.8}{s} over our range of losses, $\eta_\mathrm{g}$ from 0.5 to 0.8 and $k_r$ from 1.0 to 1.25, and \qty{2.5}{s} with no extra resistance ($\eta_\mathrm{g} = 0.7$, $k_r = 1$). The measured time falls inside that range, so the model is consistent with the measurement. One climb time cannot separate the two losses: $\eta_\mathrm{g} = 0.55$ with $k_r = 1.2$, for example, gives \qty{3.3}{s}.

Several problems in our build add to that resistance. The side guards flexed into the treads and rubbed on them in every run (Section~\ref{sec:layout}). The tracks are tensioned tightly so they do not dip and scrape at the mesa edge (Section~\ref{sec:couplers}), which loads the sprockets and axles. The printed couplers flex as they turn, the treads have friction within themselves, and the robot rubs and knocks against the side wall it uses as a guide rail.
