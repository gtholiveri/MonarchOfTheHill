\section{Drivetrain}
\label{sec:drivetrain}

The drivetrain has to carry a \qty{1}{kg} robot up the \ang{33.4} slope as fast as possible. We wanted to get up the slope faster than the kit motors would allow, so opted to purchase cheap gearmotors from Amazon. We chose optimal motors by bounding the search space with the basic physical requirements of the competition, then optimizing for predicted climb time among the constrained options.

\subsection{Upper bound on torque: traction}
\label{sec:traction-bound}

Torque the tracks cannot transmit is wasted, because past that point the tracks slip instead of pushing. The maximum force with which the tracks can push along the ramp before slipping is equal to the static friction force, $F_f$. Therefore, the total drive force $F$ is capped:
\begin{equation}
% I think equations like this are better isolated and not like multiple equations rammed into one
% I'd prefer to see these split into two separate equations, this is a general principle
  F \le \mu_s N,\text{ where } N = m g \cos\theta \text{ on the ramp and } m g \text{ on the mesa}
  \label{eq:traction}
\end{equation}
Each motor drives one track through a sprocket of \qty{50}{mm} diameter ($r = \qty{25}{mm}$), supplying half of the total drive force $F$. Therefore, the torque per motor at which the tracks start to slip is $\mu_s N r / 2$ at the sprocket. We estimated $\mu_s = 1.2$ to be the most any tread material on the ramp could reasonably give. Therefore, no motor can usefully deliver more than \qty{0.15}{N.m} at the sprocket (Table~\ref{tab:traction}).

A real drivetrain also loses torque to resistance in the tracks, axles and couplers before it reaches the ramp (Section~\ref{sec:holding-bound}), and we had no way to calculate how much without a built drivetrain. We therefore allowed stall force up to three times the traction ceiling. The factor of safety of 3 is a judgment call, not a calculation: it is our guess at the most extra torque a worst-case drivetrain could need. Torque beyond that would not be usable.

\subsection{Lower bound on torque: holding on the slope}
\label{sec:holding-bound}

Gravity exerts a force down the ramp: $m g \sin\theta = \qty{5.40}{N}$. In order to maintain constant velocity, the force the motors can provide at stall must equal at least \qty{5.4}{N}. In order to accelerate, it must be greater.
\begin{equation}
  F_\mathrm{stall} > k_r\, m g \sin\theta
  \label{eq:holding}
\end{equation}

Here $k_r \ge 1$ is a factor for resistance we cannot calculate from first principles: friction in the tracks and axles, tread tension, shaft deflection, and the side guards rubbing on the treads (Section~\ref{sec:layout}). A motor that only just clears Eq.~\eqref{eq:holding} climbs, but it has so little force to spare for acceleration that it never reaches its full speed on a \qty{1.524}{m} ramp.

\subsection{Upper bound on motor size: current}
\label{sec:current-bound}

A bigger motor makes more power only by drawing more current. A brushed DC motor with winding resistance $R$ stalls at $I_\mathrm{stall} = V / R$ and delivers its peak mechanical power at half its stall torque and half its no-load speed:
\begin{equation}
  P_\mathrm{max} = \frac{V I_\mathrm{stall}}{4}
  \label{eq:pmax}
\end{equation}
Voltage is fixed by the 2S pack in the kit. Therefore, stall current is a proxy for motor size, and we can use it to place an upper bound on motor: the largest viable motors draw current the electronics can actually supply. The peak current that a given motor can draw is its stall current. In the context of the contest, the motors will stall briefly at the start of every climb and could stall for an extended period during a pushing match. Two components limit $I_\mathrm{stall}$ (Table~\ref{tab:current}).

\paragraph{Driver}
The TB6612FNG driver we had on hand is rated for \qty{1.2}{A} continuous and \qty{3.2}{A} peak per channel~\cite{tb6612}. We reasoned that a continuous stall, though unlikely, could occur in certain scenarios, placing an upper bound on $I_\mathrm{stall}$ per motor of \qty{1.2}{A}. Realistically though, because most stall conditions are much more likely to be transient, we were comfortable choosing motors that drew more: in a brief stall, \qty{3.2}{A} is a generous allowance.

\paragraph{Pack}
The 7805 regulator providing \qty{5}{V} DC to the Photon requires its input voltage to stay above about \qty{7}{V}. Below \qty{7}{V} input, the \qty{5}{V} supply voltage cannot be sustained, and the Photon will behave erratically or cut out completely: instant loss in a match.

Therefore, the voltage sag produced by the motors in a reasonably likely, worst-case scenario must stay below that which would pull the pack below \qty{7}{V}.

The terminal voltage of a battery with an electromotive force $\mathcal{E}$ and internal resistance $R_\mathrm{int}$ will drop according to:
\begin{equation}
  V_\mathrm{term} = \mathcal{E} - I R_\mathrm{int}
  \label{eq:vterm}
\end{equation}
Therefore, with both motors at stall, the pack sags by $\Delta V_\mathrm{sag} = 2 I_\mathrm{stall} R_\mathrm{int}$.

Therefore, the right family of motor size is the motor that runs fairly efficiently at 2S voltage and won't sag the battery below the regulator minimum.

We found from the stall currents of common gearmotor sizes that a \qty{25}{mm} form factor was optimal. At \qty{6}{V}, these will stall at about \qty{2.0}{A}. At 7.4V, we estimated that some available motors on Amazon would stall at about \qty{1.5}{A}, just breaking  the worst case bound. Therefore, a 25mm gearmotor targeted to 6V is the best possible form factor for the kit battery pack.

\subsection{Choosing the gear ratio inside the bounds}
\label{sec:gear-ratio}

We found affordable \qty{25}{mm} \qty{6}{V} gearmotors on Amazon with ratios from 1:4.4 to 1:500~\cite{meccanixity}.

Varying gear ratio on the same base motor only changes how power is allocated between speed and torque - but it's strictly zero-sum with respect to total power. Therefore, choosing a gear ratio is an important tradeoff, with an optimum somewhere in the middle (Table~\ref{tab:tradeoff}).

To find where that optimum lies, we wrote the estimated climb time as a function of gear ratio, $T(G)$, and evaluated it for every version on sale.

\paragraph{Motor model}

We had to choose before we had a motor to test, so we took parameters from the listing and estimated their values at 2S voltage. The base motor's no-load speed is about \qty{6000}{\rpm} (\qty{625}{rad/s}) at \qty{6}{V}, which gives its torque constant $k$. We estimated its stall current on our \qty{7.4}{V} pack at about \qty{1.5}{A}, which sets its winding resistance $R$:
\begin{equation}
  k = \frac{\qty{6}{V}}{\qty{625}{rad/s}} = \qty{0.0096}{N.m/A}, \qquad
  R = \frac{\qty{7.4}{V}}{\qty{1.5}{A}} = \qty{4.9}{\ohm}
  \label{eq:kR}
\end{equation}

Run from the \qty{7.4}{V} pack through gear ratio $G$ with gearbox efficiency $\eta$, each motor's torque falls in a straight line from stall to no-load speed. As drive force from both tracks:
\begin{equation}
  F(v) = F_\mathrm{stall}\left(1 - \frac{v}{v_0}\right), \qquad
  F_\mathrm{stall} = \frac{2 \eta k G V}{R r}, \qquad
  v_0 = \frac{V r}{k G}
  \label{eq:motor}
\end{equation}

The drive force is capped by the traction ceiling (Eq.~\eqref{eq:traction}) and opposed by gravity and drivetrain resistance (Eq.~\eqref{eq:holding}), so along the ramp
\begin{equation}
  m \frac{dv}{dt} = \min\!\left[F(v),\ \mu_s m g \cos\theta\right] - k_r\, m g \sin\theta
  \label{eq:climb}
\end{equation}
$T(G)$ is the time Eq.~\eqref{eq:climb}, integrated from rest, takes to carry the robot \qty{1.524}{m}. It assumes full motor duty from the start, with all rolling and track losses lumped into $k_r$.

\paragraph{Applying the bounds}
Every version shares the same base motor, so all of them meet Section~\ref{sec:current-bound}. The torque bounds of Sections~\ref{sec:traction-bound} and~\ref{sec:holding-bound} leave three candidates: the 170, 280 and \qty{620}{\rpm} versions (Fig.~\ref{fig:gear}a).

\paragraph{Result}
The \qty{280}{\rpm} version (1:21.3) gives the shortest estimated climb (Fig.~\ref{fig:gear}b). It stays fastest for every combination of $\eta$ from 0.5 to 0.8 and $k_r$ from 1.0 to 1.25 except the most optimistic ($\eta = 0.8$, $k_r = 1.0$), and nothing is sold between 280 and \qty{620}{\rpm}.
