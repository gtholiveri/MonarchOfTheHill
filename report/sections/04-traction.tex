\section{Traction}
\label{sec:traction}

Section~\ref{sec:drivetrain} showed that traction caps how much of the motors' torque the robot can use, so the track surface is the most leveraged part of the drivetrain. The stock tracks are hard plastic links that provide almost no friction; with them, our robot was unable to climb at all. The final surface is strips of 80 grit sandpaper taped onto the stock tracks (Fig.~\ref{fig:tracks}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.34\textwidth]{fig05_tracks.jpg}
  \caption{Strips of 80 grit sandpaper taped onto the stock tracks.}
  \label{fig:tracks}
\end{figure}

We did not measure $\mu_s$ for the sandpaper, because no decision depended on its exact value. To hold on the ramp, the tracks only need
\begin{equation}
  \mu_s \ge \tan\ang{33.4} = 0.66
  \label{eq:mu-min}
\end{equation}
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:traction} with $N = m g \cos\ang{33.4}$ and $F = m g \sin\ang{33.4}$), and the drivetrain bounds of Section~\ref{sec:traction-bound} were already set from a realistic range of $\mu_s$. A function test settled the rest: driven up the ramp at full power, the robot showed no visible slip. We spent our analysis where the answer could not be seen by eye (Section~\ref{sec:lessons}).
