\section{Lessons learned}
\label{sec:lessons}

\paragraph{Triage, and build the minimum viable robot first}
This is the lesson that mattered most. Two ideas never got past the concept stage because they were too complex for the time we had: the servo linkage bracing against both walls and the side lifter. Several other things we knew were imperfect, and chose to live with rather than fix. We kept cut-down side guards instead of ones that fully wrapped the treads (we had the CAD, but making them work would have taken too long), the stock sprockets instead of a redesign with a controlled diameter, hot glue instead of proper electronics mounts, printed couplers instead of metal ones that would not deflect, a breadboard instead of a soldered protoboard, and one fixed wedge instead of modular ones. That left enough time to test the robot and its software, and without that testing the autonomous run would not have worked.

\paragraph{Isolate faults one connection at a time}
Our two longest debugging sessions were a breadboard jumper whose trimmed leg never seated and a cold solder joint on the IMU's power header. Both passed every check we made until we measured the voltage at each connection in turn, from the supply to the part.

\paragraph{Check stiffness as well as strength}
The side guards we cut down to fit never broke, but they flexed into the treads and dragged on them in every run. The motors had the margin to push through it (Section~\ref{sec:drivetrain-result}), but a part that bends too far can stop doing its job long before it breaks.

\paragraph{Take print anisotropy seriously}
We printed both coupler orientations and chose the upright one because the fit felt marginally nicer, and we guessed by feel that it would be strong enough, even in the anisotropically weaker configuration. For load-bearing functional parts, a guess isn't good enough: upright-printed couplers almost all broke.

\paragraph{Autonomy without positioning suffers}
The robot always reached the mesa, but it did not always carry as far across it as it should have. The climb was paced by time, so anything that slowed the robot as it went over the crest, such as vibration, track slip, or an opponent, changed where it stopped. Wheel or motor encoders would not fix this, because they cannot tell when the tracks slip. An absolute position reference would: a time-of-flight sensor ranging to a beacon, or a camera such as an ESP32-CAM locating AprilTags on the side walls. We had hoped to build one but ran out of time.

Timing also hides slow changes in the robot. The treads lost tension over successive runs, which we could feel happening. The slack came not from the axle slots but from the couplers, which had bent visibly, by roughly 2 to \ang{5} judging from memory. A plausible reason the robot stopped short in the final round is that slack treads matter most going over the crest. The treads are tensioned so that the robot does not bottom out there (Section~\ref{sec:layout}); once they are slack, the bottom of the robot can drop onto the edge. That contact takes normal force off the treads and adds friction, briefly slowing the robot as it crosses onto the mesa, so it carries less far in the same fixed time, and nothing in a timed run can notice.

\paragraph{Spend analysis where the eye cannot see}
Many questions answered themselves by inspection: whether the tracks slipped, whether the robot tipped, whether it stopped on the mesa. We judged or tuned those by eye, which was good enough, and saved more rigorous analysis for tradeoffs that cannot be eyeballed, such as the gear ratio (Section~\ref{sec:gear-ratio}) and battery voltage sag (Section~\ref{sec:current-bound}) \tbd{finish this list}.
