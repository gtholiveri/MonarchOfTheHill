\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{The contest}
\label{sec:contest}

The Monarch of the Hill contest is a one-on-one match between autonomous robots on a hill of two \qty{5}{ft} (\qty{1.524}{m}) ramps that meet at a flat \qty{1}{ft} by \qty{1}{ft} mesa \qty{33}{in} above the floor (Fig.~\ref{fig:hill}). The ramps are \qty{1}{ft} wide between \qty{1}{in} guide rails and covered with a non-slip surface like a bathtub mat. Their angle is
\begin{equation}
  \theta = \arcsin\frac{\qty{33}{in}}{\qty{60}{in}} = \ang{33.4}
  \label{eq:theta}
\end{equation}

Each robot starts on the slope at the bottom of its ramp, with its front no more than \qty{1}{ft} up the ramp, and a judge starts it with a single switch on the robot. After \qty{30}{s}, the robot whose farthest point is closest, in three dimensions, to the center of the mesa wins; a robot still on the hill beats one that has fallen off. The rules cap the robot at \qty{1}{kg}, require it to fit inside a \qty{1}{ft} cube at the start, forbid remote control, and limit stored energy to the supplied two-cell (2S) 18650 lithium-ion pack plus small springs. Groups play 5 to 6 rounds in a Swiss-system tournament~\cite{ronney}.

\subsection{Strategy}
\label{sec:strategy}

Our objective was to reach the mesa first and keep the opponent from displacing us for the rest of the \qty{30}{s}. Three things made arriving first crucial.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{An attacker on the slope has far less force to push with.} Part of its traction holds the robot up the slope against gravity, $m g \sin\ang{33.4}$, and the traction is capped by the normal force, which on the slope is only $m g \cos\ang{33.4}$. The most an attacker can push with, against the most a defender on the mesa can resist with, is
  \begin{align}
    F_\mathrm{att} &= \mu_s m g \cos\ang{33.4} - m g \sin\ang{33.4} \label{eq:attack} \\
    F_\mathrm{def} &= \mu_s m g \label{eq:defend}
  \end{align}
  For two robots of equal mass and any $\mu_s$ from 0.8 to 1.2, the defender can resist 2.7 to 6.8 times the force the attacker can apply. This is an estimate for the attacker fully on the ramp and the defender fully on the mesa; while the attacker bridges the crest the forces lie between.
  \item \textbf{An attacker arrives with its wedge in the air.} Its wedge cannot sit flush on the mesa until the robot has fully cleared the ramp, so its edge is most likely still raised when it meets the defender's wedge.
  \item \textbf{Autonomy favors the defender.} Until the robots meet, a robot's path is predictable. After contact, a robot without absolute positioning or vision knows very little about where it is, so escaping a losing position is largely infeasible. A robot already on top needs very little information to hold its place.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Defense without a pushing match}
We planned never to have to out-push an opponent, because getting under an opponent is far more effective than pushing it, for two reasons.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A lifted opponent cannot push.} Getting under it takes weight off its tracks. Less normal force means less traction and less force to push with (Eq.~\eqref{eq:attack}).
  \item \textbf{Height counts as much as distance.} The winner is decided by the three-dimensional distance from the mesa center to each robot's farthest point, so lifting an opponent raises its farthest point just as shoving it forward would.
\end{itemize}
Together these make the ground game, the contact between the two robots at the mesa, the most important part of winning.

\subsection{High-level mechanical design}
\label{sec:concept}

\paragraph{Wedge and forks}
We borrowed a low wedge with forks from combat robotics. The forks matter because no wedge can be perfectly flush with the ground, given manufacturing tolerances and strain in operation, and trying would leave the wedge dragging on the high-friction ramp. In a flat arena, forks sit flush almost all the time under gravity, and their mass and contact area are so small that the friction they add is negligible. For the three-dimensional hill we made two adaptations (Section~\ref{sec:wedge}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Forks that stay flush while cresting.} We could not count on reaching the mesa first every time, so the forks stay flush with the mesa even while the robot is still cresting onto it.
  \item \textbf{A wedge at the angle of the ramp.} The wedge nearly matches the slope of the ramp, so an opponent that drives up onto it sees no change in pitch and does not detect that it has reached the crest.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The walls as guide rails}
We wanted the robot stable and hard to displace in every direction, so its frame fits the full width of the ramp and uses the walls as guide rails. It cannot become misaligned, shoved sideways or pitched over the side.

These goals set three requirements that organize the rest of the report: climb as fast as traction and available power allow (Section~\ref{sec:drivetrain}); end the climb at the front of the mesa (Sections~\ref{sec:sensing} and~\ref{sec:control}); and hold position there, relying on the wedge and forks to preempt most pushing matches (Sections~\ref{sec:wedge} and~\ref{sec:run}).
