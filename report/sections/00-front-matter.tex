\section*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}
We designed and built an autonomous tracked robot for the AME 101 Monarch of the Hill contest. Two robots start at the bottoms of opposite \ang{33.4} ramps, and after \qty{30}{s} the robot whose farthest point is closest to the center of the shared mesa wins. Our strategy was to win the ground game: reach the mesa first, and never depend on a pushing match. A low wedge with forks at the front gets under an arriving opponent, which then drives up and over us or off the side, and a frame as wide as the ramp uses both walls as guide rails. We won the contest with a 5--1 record, and four of the five wins resulted from ground game. To reach the mesa first, we chose the drive motors by bounding the options by traction, by the force needed to climb and by the current the electronics can supply, then modeling climb time as a function of gear ratio. This picked a \qty{25}{mm}, \qty{280}{\rpm} gearmotor. Its measured stall current, \qty{1.4}{A}, matched our \qty{1.5}{A} estimate, and its measured climb, about \qty{3.3}{s}, falls inside the model's range of 2.4 to \qty{3.8}{s}. A complementary filter that fuses gyro and accelerometer readings kept the pitch estimate within \ang{4.5} of the true angle while climbing, where the accelerometer alone was off by up to \ang{122}. Our one loss, in the final round, came from the robot's timed advance onto the mesa: it stopped short, and the opponent's farthest point ended up closer to the center. Our main lesson is to triage hard: dropping complex ideas early left enough time to test the robot and its software.
