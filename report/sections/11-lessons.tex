\section{Lessons learned}
\label{sec:lessons}

\paragraph{Triage, and build the minimum viable robot first}
This mattered most. We dropped the servo wall brace and the side lifter as too complex for the time we had, and chose to live with several known imperfections rather than fix them: cut-down side guards instead of ones that fully wrapped the treads, the stock sprockets instead of a redesign with a controlled diameter, hot glue instead of proper electronics mounts, printed couplers instead of metal ones that would not deflect, a breadboard instead of a soldered protoboard, and one fixed wedge instead of modular ones. That left enough time to test the robot and its software, and without that testing the autonomous run would not have worked.

\paragraph{Isolate faults one connection at a time}
Our two longest debugging sessions were a trimmed ground jumper whose leg was too short to seat, which took about three hours, and cold solder joints on the IMU's power header pins, which took about five. Both connections looked fine, and we found the jumper only by accident. Probing each connection in turn with a multimeter, from the supply to the part, would have found both directly.

\paragraph{Check stiffness as well as strength}
The side guards we cut down to fit never broke, but they flexed into the treads and dragged on them in every run. The motors pushed through it at the cost of climb time (Section~\ref{sec:drivetrain-result}), but a part that bends too far can stop doing its job long before it breaks.

\paragraph{Take print anisotropy seriously}
We printed both coupler orientations and chose the upright one because the fit felt marginally nicer, and we guessed by feel that it would be strong enough. For load-bearing functional parts a guess is not good enough: the upright couplers almost all broke. A few more test prints, with the lying print's bore compensated for sag, would have given us the close fit and the strength together.

\paragraph{Autonomy without positioning suffers}
The robot always reached the mesa, but it did not always carry as far across it as it should have. Once the climb ends, on the crest or on the timer, the advance onto the mesa is open loop: a fixed \qty{150}{ms} power ramp and the robot's momentum carry it forward, and Hold drives forward only if the nose is still up (Section~\ref{sec:run}). Anything that slows the robot as it goes over the edge, such as vibration, track slip or an opponent, shortens that advance. Wheel or motor encoders would not fix this, because they cannot tell when the tracks slip. An absolute position reference would: a time-of-flight sensor ranging to a beacon, or a camera such as an ESP32-CAM locating AprilTags on the side walls. We had hoped to build one but ran out of time.

Timing also hides slow changes in the robot. The treads lost tension over successive runs, which we could feel happening, and the slack came from the couplers, which had bent visibly, by roughly 2 to \ang{5} from memory. A plausible reason the robot stopped short in the final round is that slack treads matter most going over the crest. The treads are tensioned so that the robot does not bottom out there (Section~\ref{sec:layout}); once they are slack, the bottom of the robot can drop onto the edge, which takes normal force off the treads and adds friction. That briefly slows the robot as it crosses onto the mesa, so it carries less far in the same timed advance, and nothing in the run can notice.

\paragraph{Spend analysis where the eye cannot see}
Many questions answered themselves by inspection: whether the tracks slipped, whether the robot tipped, whether it stopped on the mesa. We judged or tuned those by eye, which was good enough, and saved rigorous analysis and testing for what cannot be eyeballed:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the print clearances, which we found by printing sweeps of test fits (Section~\ref{sec:layout});
  \item the gear ratio (Section~\ref{sec:gear-ratio});
  \item the motors' current limits (Section~\ref{sec:current-bound});
  \item how far an accelerometer alone misreads pitch while the robot accelerates, and how well the filter fixes it (Sections~\ref{sec:accel-only} and~\ref{sec:pitch-test});
  \item why the couplers broke (Section~\ref{sec:couplers}).
\end{itemize}
