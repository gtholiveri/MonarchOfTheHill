\section{Sensing the crest}
\label{sec:sensing}

\subsection{Why an accelerometer alone is not enough}
\label{sec:accel-only}

The robot has to know when it has crossed from the \ang{33.4} ramp onto the flat mesa, and it has to know this while speeding up and slowing down. An accelerometer reads acceleration along three basis vectors. At standstill, the resultant acceleration vector it reads is the gravity vector, from which it's trivial to calculate the robot's tilt: simply take the angle between the robot's up direction $\hat{\bm{z}}$ and the measured vector $\bm{a}_\mathrm{m}$,
\begin{equation}
  \theta = \arccos\frac{\bm{a}_\mathrm{m} \cdot \hat{\bm{z}}}{|\bm{a}_\mathrm{m}|}
  \label{eq:tilt}
\end{equation}
However, when the robot accelerates along the ramp, the resultant vector ceases to be the gravity vector.

Along the ramp it reads $g \sin\ang{33.4} + a$ and across it $g \cos\ang{33.4}$, so the pitch it implies is
\begin{equation}
  \theta_\mathrm{apparent} = \arctan\!\left(\frac{g \sin\ang{33.4} + a}{g \cos\ang{33.4}}\right)
  \label{eq:apparent}
\end{equation}
Decelerating at \qty{3}{m/s^2}, for example, makes the ramp look like \ang{16}, below the \ang{18} threshold at which the autopilot treats the robot as crested. Fig.~\ref{fig:pitch} shows how large this error was in our climbs.

Acceleration along the ramp does not touch the reading in the perpendicular direction, so in principle pitch could come from that component alone, $\theta = \arccos(\bm{a}_\mathrm{m} \cdot \hat{\bm{z}} / g)$. That component is not immune to vibration, though: three times in our climbs the accelerometer-only pitch read past \ang{90} (Fig.~\ref{fig:pitch}), which means the measured component along $\hat{\bm{z}}$ briefly swung negative, a jolt of more than $g \cos\ang{33.4} = \qty{8.2}{m/s^2}$.

A gyroscope measures rotation rate directly, so it is more resilient to vibration and is not confused by acceleration along the ramp. However, turning its rate into an angle means integrating, and integration accumulates error. Ours read up to \qty{4.7}{\degree/s} with the robot at rest; over a \qty{5}{s} climb that offset alone would add about \ang{24} of error.

\subsection{Complementary filter}
\label{sec:filter}

% clean up my flow but this is more what I want. It's close
We wrote a simple filter to enable the robot to accurately estimate its tilt. The algorithm is essentially that when we know the accelerometer is being shaken or accelerated, we do not trust it. The rest of the time, it's used as a constant correction source for the gyro's drift.

We chose this approach because it was straightforward and required little additional tuning or analysis. We didn't have to write or tune smoothing functions, or find frequencies to filter out. Our testing in Section~\ref{sec:pitch-test} shows that the approach, though simple, was very effective.

The robot keeps its estimate of which way is up as a unit vector $\hat{\bm{u}}$ in the sensor's frame, and updates it every \qty{20}{ms} in two steps. First, it rotates $\hat{\bm{u}}$ by how far the gyro says the robot turned since the last update. Second, if the accelerometer is trusted, it moves $\hat{\bm{u}}$ a small step toward the direction the accelerometer reads.

Each correction step closes $\Delta t / T_c$ of the gap, where $\Delta t = \qty{20}{ms}$ is the update interval and $T_c = \qty{1.5}{s}$ is the time constant. That is about \qty{1.3}{\percent} of the gap per update. Over a second or two, a trusted accelerometer removes the gyro's drift, but a single noisy reading barely moves the estimate.

With the gyro rate $\bm{\omega}$ and the accelerometer reading $\bm{a}_\mathrm{m}$, the first step is
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\bm{u}} \leftarrow \operatorname{normalize}\!\big(\hat{\bm{u}} - (\bm{\omega} \times \hat{\bm{u}})\,\Delta t\big)
  \label{eq:gyro-step}
\end{equation}
and the second step is
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\bm{u}} \leftarrow \operatorname{normalize}\!\left(\hat{\bm{u}} + \frac{\Delta t}{T_c}\left(\frac{\bm{a}_\mathrm{m}}{|\bm{a}_\mathrm{m}|} - \hat{\bm{u}}\right)\right)
  \label{eq:accel-step}
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{normalize}$ rescales a vector to unit length.

The accelerometer is trusted only while its reading is within \qty{8}{\percent} of \qty{1}{g}, the rotation rate is below \qty{30}{\degree/s}, and the power applied to the motors has not changed for \qty{0.6}{s}. At every other moment the estimate follows the gyro alone.

The gyro offset is averaged over 50 readings with the robot at rest and subtracted from every reading after that. A two-step calibration (robot level, then nose up) gives the robot its own up and forward directions, from which it computes pitch.

\subsection{Requirement and test}
\label{sec:pitch-test}

The autopilot treats the robot as crested when the pitch stays below \ang{18} for \qty{40}{ms}, and on the mesa it counts anything within \ang{\pm 6} as level. The estimate therefore must not read more than \ang{15} low while climbing the \ang{33.4} ramp, and must be within \ang{\pm 6} at rest.
To test this we used the hill itself as ground truth: anywhere on the ramp the true pitch is \ang{33.4}, and on the mesa it is \ang{0}. We logged both the accelerometer-only and the fused pitch through three full autonomous runs (Fig.~\ref{fig:pitch}, Table~\ref{tab:pitch}). Climbing, the accelerometer alone was off by up to \ang{122}, while the fused estimate never read more than \ang{4.5} from the true angle, well inside the \ang{15} the crest test allows. At rest, both were within \ang{4} on the mesa, inside the \ang{\pm 6} level band.
