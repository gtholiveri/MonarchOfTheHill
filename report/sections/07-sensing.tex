\section{Sensing the crest}
\label{sec:sensing}

\subsection{Why an accelerometer alone is not enough}
\label{sec:accel-only}

The robot uses its pitch twice: to end the climb if it crests before the timer runs out, and to hold its place on the mesa when it is shoved or moved (Section~\ref{sec:run}). Both happen while it speeds up and slows down. An accelerometer reads acceleration along three axes. At standstill the vector it reads is the gravity vector, so the robot's tilt is simply the angle between its up direction $\hat{\bm{z}}$ and the measured vector $\bm{a}_\mathrm{m}$:
\begin{equation}
  \theta = \arccos\frac{\bm{a}_\mathrm{m} \cdot \hat{\bm{z}}}{|\bm{a}_\mathrm{m}|}
  \label{eq:tilt}
\end{equation}
When the robot accelerates along the ramp, however, the measured vector is no longer the gravity vector. Along the ramp the accelerometer reads $g \sin\ang{33.4} + a$ and across it $g \cos\ang{33.4}$, so the pitch it implies is
\begin{equation}
  \theta_\mathrm{apparent} = \arctan\!\left(\frac{g \sin\ang{33.4} + a}{g \cos\ang{33.4}}\right)
  \label{eq:apparent}
\end{equation}
Decelerating at \qty{3}{m/s^2}, for example, makes the ramp look like \ang{16}, below the \ang{18} crest threshold (Section~\ref{sec:run}). Fig.~\ref{fig:pitch} shows how large this error was in our climbs.

Using only the component perpendicular to the ramp, Eq.~\eqref{eq:tilt} with $g$ in place of $|\bm{a}_\mathrm{m}|$, would avoid the along-ramp acceleration, but that component is not immune to vibration. On the ramp at rest the accelerometer's $z$ axis reads $\cos\ang{33.4} = \qty{0.83}{g}$, and three times in our climbs it dropped below zero, to as low as \qty{-0.33}{g} (Fig.~\ref{fig:pitch}, Appendix~\ref{app:data}).

A gyroscope measures rotation rate directly, so it resists vibration and is not confused by acceleration along the ramp. But turning its rate into an angle means integrating, and integration accumulates error. Ours read up to \qty{4.7}{\degree/s} at rest. Left uncorrected, that offset alone would add about \ang{16} of error over a \qty{3.3}{s} climb. We subtract a measured offset at startup (Section~\ref{sec:filter}), but no offset correction is exact, so the integrated angle still drifts.

\subsection{Complementary filter}
\label{sec:filter}

We wrote a simple filter to estimate the robot's tilt. Whenever the accelerometer is being shaken or accelerated, the filter ignores it; the rest of the time, the accelerometer continuously corrects the gyro's drift. We chose this approach because it needed little tuning, with no smoothing functions to write or frequencies to find, and the testing in Section~\ref{sec:pitch-test} shows that, though simple, it was effective.

The robot keeps its estimate of which way is up as a unit vector $\hat{\bm{u}}$ in the sensor's frame and updates it every \qty{20}{ms} in two steps. First, it rotates $\hat{\bm{u}}$ by how far the gyro says the robot turned since the last update. Second, if the accelerometer is trusted, it moves $\hat{\bm{u}}$ a small step toward the direction the accelerometer reads. With the gyro rate $\bm{\omega}$ and the accelerometer reading $\bm{a}_\mathrm{m}$, the first step is
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\bm{u}} \leftarrow \operatorname{normalize}\!\big(\hat{\bm{u}} - (\bm{\omega} \times \hat{\bm{u}})\,\Delta t\big)
  \label{eq:gyro-step}
\end{equation}
and the second step is
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\bm{u}} \leftarrow \operatorname{normalize}\!\left(\hat{\bm{u}} + \frac{\Delta t}{T_c}\left(\frac{\bm{a}_\mathrm{m}}{|\bm{a}_\mathrm{m}|} - \hat{\bm{u}}\right)\right)
  \label{eq:accel-step}
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{normalize}$ rescales a vector to unit length. Each correction step closes $\Delta t / T_c$ of the gap, with $\Delta t = \qty{20}{ms}$ the update interval and $T_c = \qty{1.5}{s}$ the time constant. That is about \qty{1.3}{\percent} of the gap per update, so over a second or two a trusted accelerometer removes the gyro's drift, but a single noisy reading barely moves the estimate. The accelerometer is trusted only while its reading is within \qty{8}{\percent} of \qty{1}{g}, the rotation rate is below \qty{30}{\degree/s}, and the power applied to the motors has not changed for \qty{0.6}{s}; at every other moment the estimate follows the gyro alone. The gyro offset is averaged over 50 readings at rest and subtracted from every later reading, and a two-step calibration (robot level, then nose up) gives the robot its own up and forward directions, from which it computes pitch.

\subsection{Requirement and test}
\label{sec:pitch-test}

The autopilot treats the robot as crested when the pitch stays below \ang{18} for \qty{40}{ms}, and on the mesa it counts anything within \ang{\pm 6} as level. The estimate therefore must not read more than \ang{15} low while climbing the \ang{33.4} ramp, and must be within \ang{\pm 6} at rest.

To test this we used the hill itself as ground truth: anywhere on the ramp the true pitch is \ang{33.4}, and on the mesa it is \ang{0}. We logged both the accelerometer-only and the fused pitch through three full autonomous runs (Fig.~\ref{fig:pitch}, Table~\ref{tab:pitch}). Climbing, the accelerometer alone was off by up to \ang{122}, while the fused estimate never read more than \ang{4.5} from the true angle, well inside the \ang{15} the crest test allows. At rest, both were within \ang{4} on the mesa, inside the \ang{\pm 6} level band. We left Run C out of the rest rows: in it both the accelerometer and the fused estimate read about \ang{3} below the known angle at rest, on the ramp and on the mesa alike, so the robot itself sat about \ang{3} off. The fused estimate's larger error at rest on the mesa is left over from the crest: it arrives up to \ang{4} off, and once the robot is still, the accelerometer pulls it to within \ang{1} in about \qty{3}{s}.
