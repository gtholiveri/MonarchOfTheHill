\section{Design concepts and selection}
\label{sec:concepts}

We considered only a handful of alternatives and dropped each quickly. A hinged wedge, passive or spring-loaded, would have kept the wedge edge on the ramp surface, but fixed forks do the same job more simply and better (Section~\ref{sec:wedge}). A servo linkage bracing against both walls, and a side lifter to tip an opponent over the guide rail, were both too complex for the time we had. Tennis grip tape wrapped continuously around the tracks would have raised traction, but it slipped off: a continuous loop driven like a belt stays in place only on real pulleys, and our tracks deflect and are unconstrained at the sides.

We built a tracked robot with a forks-and-wedge front end that spans the full width of the ramp (Fig.~\ref{fig:robot}). The front end has no moving parts: an opponent that drives into it climbs onto the forks and wedge, which takes weight off its tracks and with it the traction it needs to push (Section~\ref{sec:strategy}). In some cases the wedge also sends the opponent off the side.
